🎧 Theory of Change - AI Governance

Overarching Theory of Change​

Current AI governance approaches analyze systems in isolation, missing crucial emergent behaviors. Technical frameworks predominantly analyze AI systems as individual components rather than interconnected entities within complex networks. Academic research focuses on theoretical risks like misalignment, while industry develops responsible AI practices that apply primarily to single systems. Most regulatory frameworks typically mandate documentation requirements and auditing procedures that examine systems in isolation. (Reuel et al., 2025, Open Problems in Technical AI Governance).

This isolated assessment creates blind spots for emergent behaviors and system-level effects. Current approaches cannot adequately model how individual AI behaviors cascade through networks to create unexpected collective dynamics. Existing tools fail to capture cross-scale effects where small interventions might have outsized impacts. Additionally, there's no reliable way to verify whether important properties like information integrity or power balance will be preserved as systems evolve and interact. (Reuel et al., 2025, Open Problems in Technical AI Governance).

Equilibria addresses these gaps through three interconnected approaches - Stakeholder Networks, Agent Based Simulations, and Mathematical Foundations. We build Stakeholder Networks to identify concrete coordination problems, develop System Simulations to model interaction effects, and research Mathematical Foundations to accurately represent complex network dynamics. This integrated approach connects stakeholder insights with computational modeling and mathematical rigor, helping decision-makers understand how governance interventions might play out across interconnected AI systems.

This document focuses specifically on our Stakeholder Networks approach, examining how it helps bridge the gap between theoretical governance ideals and practical implementation.

Governance Stakeholder Networks - Theory of Change​

There is a theory-practice gap causing theoretical governance frameworks to regularly fail during practical implementation. Imagine you're a policymaker who has crafted regulations requiring "comprehensive documentation of training data" based on excellent academic research. When you consult with AI developers on implementation, practical questions emerge that your theoretical framework doesn't address: What specific documentation satisfies this requirement? How can transparency be balanced with intellectual property concerns? What audit procedures can verify claims without revealing proprietary methods? These practical challenges aren't addressed in academic papers or theoretical frameworks, yet they determine whether governance succeeds or fails in practice.

This theory-practice gap exists because governance frameworks lack structured translation mechanisms. When policymakers require "explainable AI," this abstract principle doesn't specify what documentation developers should produce, what metrics auditors should evaluate, or what evidence regulators should require. Each stakeholder interprets the requirement differently, leading to misaligned implementation. We've seen this pattern before—when cybersecurity frameworks were initially developed, organizations struggled to translate high-level principles into specific technical controls and processes. AI governance faces the same challenge, but with higher stakes and greater complexity.

We bridge the theory-practice gap by creating stakeholder networks as structured environments where this translation work happens systematically. We organize and facilitate structured dialogues between diverse stakeholders who must collectively implement governance frameworks. These networks serve as the mechanism through which abstract principles are converted into specific, implementable requirements through a deliberate translation process. For example, a network focused on model documentation requirements would bring together AI developers, auditors, and policymakers to develop shared technical specifications that satisfy regulatory intent while addressing practical constraints.

The networks engage specific stakeholders who face concrete coordination challenges:

  • AI developers navigating complex safety-capability tradeoffs
  • Corporate governance teams implementing responsible AI practices
  • Regulatory officials crafting enforceable standards
  • Civil society organizations representing affected communities
  • Security researchers identifying emerging vulnerabilities

Our engagement process follows a structured methodology that moves from problem identification to practical implementation guidance. First, we identify specific governance challenges where translation gaps exist through preliminary stakeholder conversations. Then, we convene targeted working groups that systematically document different perspectives and requirements. Finally, we facilitate the collaborative development of translation frameworks that bridge these perspectives, resulting in concrete guidance documents, specification templates, or technical standards that stakeholders can implement.

This systematic engagement approach provides three key benefits:

  • Problem Relevance: We focus our analytical tools on real challenges identified by practitioners, ensuring our work addresses pressing governance needs.
  • Implementation Insight: We incorporate practical constraints and incentives from the beginning, enriching theoretical approaches with implementation context.
  • Adaptive Framework: We maintain ongoing dialogue as technology and institutions evolve, allowing governance approaches to adapt to changing circumstances.

This approach enhances existing multi-stakeholder forums by focusing specifically on translation between principles and implementation. Organizations like the Partnership on AI bring diverse perspectives together, but these discussions often remain at the level of principles and values. Our structured approach focuses specifically on developing the missing translation layer between high-level principles and technical implementation—where governance actually succeeds or fails in practice. Rather than replacing these forums, our work complements them by addressing the critical gap between aspiration and implementation.

The practical value is clearer governance implementation with fewer unintended consequences. By addressing the concrete problems stakeholders themselves identify, our approach creates natural incentives for adoption. By incorporating practical constraints from the beginning, it ensures solutions are actually implementable. And by maintaining ongoing dialogue as technology evolves, it helps governance approaches adapt to changing circumstances—making AI governance more effective in real-world contexts.

This stakeholder work directly connects to our complex systems modeling approach. When stakeholders identify specific coordination challenges, these insights shape the scenarios we model in our simulation frameworks and the properties we capture in our mathematical foundations. For example, when AI lab safety teams describe difficulties in sharing safety findings while maintaining competitive positioning, we model various information-sharing mechanisms and their effects on both safety outcomes and market dynamics. This integration ensures our analytical frameworks address concrete implementation challenges rather than theoretical abstractions—strengthening the connection between stakeholder concerns and systemic analysis.
